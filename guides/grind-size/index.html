Coffee grind size explained

Finer for short brews, coarser for long ones. Here's how to pick a grind and fix a cup by changing it.

Grind size is the biggest lever you have over how coffee tastes. Water pulls flavor out of coffee, and how quickly that happens depends on how fine the grounds are. Finer grounds give water more surface to work on, so the coffee extracts faster. Coarser grounds extract slower. Once you know that, you can fix most bad cups by changing one thing.

The basic idea

The shorter the brew, the finer the grind. The longer the brew, the coarser the grind. Espresso takes about 30 seconds and uses a fine grind. A cold brew steeps for 12 to 24 hours and uses a coarse one.

Grind sizes by method

Extra fine
Turkish coffee. Powder-like.
Fine
Espresso. Finer than table salt.
Medium-fine
Pour-over, AeroPress, and moka pot (some guides put the moka pot slightly finer). About like table salt to sand.
Medium
Automatic drip machines. About like kosher salt.
Coarse
French press and percolator. About like sea salt or coarse breadcrumbs.
Extra coarse
Cold brew. About like coarse sand or cracked peppercorns.

These are rough ranges. Published charts disagree on exact particle sizes, and no standards-body chart was found, so use this as a starting point and adjust by taste.

Our guides give a starting grind for each method: pour-over, french press, percolator, moka pot, AeroPress, drip machine, cold brew, and espresso.

Fix a bad cup by changing the grind

Change only one thing at a time, and change the grind a little at a time.

  • Sour, thin, or salty: under-extracted. Grind finer.
  • Bitter, harsh, or dry: over-extracted. Grind coarser.
  • Both sour and bitter at once: this can point to an uneven grinder, where some grounds are too fine and others too coarse.

Burr or blade grinder

A blade grinder chops beans with a spinning blade, like a small blender. It makes pieces of all sizes, from dust to chunks, so the coffee extracts unevenly and tastes both sour and bitter. A burr grinder crushes beans between two surfaces set a fixed distance apart, so the pieces come out much more even. It also lets you set the grind size and repeat it.

  • If you can afford one upgrade, a burr grinder is the one. Several coffee retailers argue it matters more than any other piece of gear.
  • Burrs come in flat and conical shapes. Both work well, and the differences matter most to enthusiasts.
  • Be wary of very cheap "burr" grinders. Prima Coffee warns that some produce uneven grounds.
  • For a first burr grinder, hand grinders and entry-level electric ones are both fine. Prices change, so check current ones.

Tips that make a bigger difference than gear

  • Grind right before you brew. Ground coffee goes stale within minutes, not days.
  • Write down your setting. Grinders have different numbers. Note the setting that worked for each method and each bag.
  • Expect to adjust. A new bag, a different roast level, or older beans can all change the right setting.

This guide describes the general rule, not exact numbers. Grinders, beans, and brewers all vary. Based on published guides, including Beanbox, MasterClass, Clive Coffee, and Prima Coffee.
